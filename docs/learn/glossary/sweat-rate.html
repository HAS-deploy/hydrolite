sweat rate — hydration glossary

What it means

Sweat rate is the volume of fluid lost through sweating over a given period of time, typically expressed in liters per hour (L/hr) or milliliters per minute. It reflects how quickly your body is losing water — and electrolytes — through the skin during exercise, heat exposure, or physical stress. Individual sweat rates vary widely, influenced by factors like exercise intensity, ambient temperature and humidity, fitness level, body size, and genetics.

Measuring sweat rate is straightforward in principle: weigh yourself (in minimal clothing) before and after exercise, account for any fluids consumed and urine lost during that period, and the difference represents approximate fluid loss. Each kilogram of body weight lost equals roughly one liter of sweat.

Why it matters

Knowing your sweat rate is the foundation of a practical hydration strategy. If you're losing fluid faster than you're replacing it, performance and cognitive function can decline — even a 2% drop in body weight from sweat loss is enough to impair endurance performance in many people. Conversely, drinking far more than you sweat can lead to overhydration.

Sweat rate becomes especially relevant for endurance athletes (runners, cyclists, triathletes), outdoor workers in hot environments, and anyone training through summer heat. Sports dietitians and coaches use sweat rate data to build personalized drinking plans — how much to drink per hour, what electrolyte concentration to target, and how to adjust for changing conditions.

Example

A runner weighs 70 kg before a one-hour training run and 69.2 kg afterward. She drank 500 mL of water during the run. Her sweat loss was approximately 800 mL (the 800 g weight drop) plus the 500 mL she drank, totaling roughly 1.3 L. Her sweat rate for that session was approximately 1.3 L/hr.

Common confusions

Sweat rate vs. sweat composition: Sweat rate tells you how much fluid you're losing; it says nothing about the sodium, potassium, or other electrolytes in that fluid. Someone with a moderate sweat rate but very salty sweat may need more electrolyte replacement than someone with a higher sweat rate and dilute sweat.

Sweat rate vs. hydration status: A high sweat rate doesn't automatically mean you're dehydrated — it just means you're losing fluid quickly. Whether you become dehydrated depends on how well your intake keeps up with losses.

Sweat rate as fixed: Many people assume their sweat rate is constant, but it shifts session to session based on temperature, humidity, effort level, and even acclimatization. A sweat rate measured on a cool morning may be significantly lower than one measured on a humid afternoon at the same pace.
